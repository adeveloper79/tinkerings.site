## 1. The Setup: "I Just Wanted a Small, Crisp Screen..."

Every rabbit hole starts with an innocent purchase. 

I was working on a compact project of using my Raspberry Pi Zero 2W as a retro handheld, and needed a small, high-density display. Browsing an electronics supplier, I stumbled upon the [GoldenMorning GMT397-01](https://goldenmorninglcd.com/tft-display/3.97-inch-480x800-st7701-gmt397-01/) a 3.97-inch IPS panel with a sharp 480x800 resolution. It looked perfect.

What I didn't know at the time was that high-resolution smartphone-style panels are not panels like ILI9341s and ST7789Vs. You can't just wire up and run them directly from Raspberry pis. Behind the glass sits a **Sitronix ST7701** display controller. Before the ST7701's internal charge pumps and analog TFT gate drivers will even begin steering liquid crystals, it demands a complex, multi-page sequence of initialization commands over a specialized **9-bit serial interface (SPI)**. Without those commands, it’s just an inert slab of glass.

To drive it over standard HDMI, I ordered an [Alibaba "Smart Display" board based on the Realtek RTD2660H](https://www.alibaba.com/product-detail/Smart-Display-2-8-4-0_1601598688378.html).

## 2. Hardware Carnage: Snapped Traces and Kapton Tape

When the Alibaba board finally arrived, I plugged in the display that came with it. The picture worked, but it had bad overscan issues borders cut off, text clipped. 

![The stock display running on the Alibaba board showing bad overscan and clipped borders](assets/overscan.jpg)


Naturally, I unplugged that panel and plugged in my original GoldenMorning ST7701 panel to see if it scaled properly. It showed color differences, inverted colors, and the exact same overscan. 

![GoldenMorning 3.97" Panel showing color inversion and overscan](assets/goldenmorning_inverted.jpg)

![GoldenMorning 3.97" Panel angle view](assets/goldenmorning_inverted2.jpg)

Then came a painful lesson in display hardware fragility: the panel had a weak FPC cable. After just two gentle bends, **the microscopic conductive traces said bye bye off the glass interface**. I threw it under a microscope hoping to bodge solder it, but the traces had ripped directly from the transparent ITO (indium tin oxide) pads on the glass. It was unrecoverable.

![The fragile FPC ribbon cable](assets/FPC.jpg)

![The snapped FPC torn clean off the glass substrate](assets/FPC2_wthPanel.jpg)

Refusing to let the project die on day two, I immediately ordered another panel: **ST7701S**. thinking it would be an identical drop-in replacement: the [DWIN 4.3 Inch 480x800](https://www.dwin-global.com/4-3-inch-480x800-rgb-interface-ips-tft-lcd-li48800t043tc3098-product/).

*It wasn't.*

When it arrived, the pinout of that panel was inverted in key places:
1. **SDA and CS pins were swapped**.
2. **One pin was connected directly to a 3.3V rail from the board** that threatened to fry the panel's logic or pull-up circuit.

Out came the microscope, fine tweezers, and a roll of heat-resistant **Kapton tape**. I sliced hairline strips of Kapton tape to isolate the 3.3V contact pad on the connector, used two breakout boards of 40-pin FPC with long FPC cables *ordered again*, and ran jumper wires to cross the SDA and CS lines, and plugged it in.

![Dual 40-pin FPC breakout boards with jumper wires crossing SDA/CS and custom wiring](assets/FPC_breakout.jpg)

![Kapton tape placed to isolate the 3.3V pin](assets/kapton.jpg)

![Microscope macro closeup of the Kapton tape isolating the 3.3V contact pad](assets/kapton_closeup.jpg)

But no, it didn't output anything except just backlight.

## 3. The Vendor Wall and the Open-Source Drought

I quickly realized the generic firmware on the board wasn't going to cut it. I needed to adjust timings, fix overscan, calibrate colors, and get proper 60Hz video.

I reached out to the Alibaba seller, politely asking for source code. 
**The seller sadly rejected.**

Then had to sort back to GitHub and forums. Surely someone in the open-source community had written firmware for RTD2660?

*Few had BUT....*
Every existing open-source RTD2660 repository (`ORTD2662`, `danyaPostfactum/RTD2660`, etc.) targeted either:
- **LVDS panels** (standard laptop and desktop monitor panels).
- **Direct TTL panels** (like the classic AT070TN92) that take raw parallel RGB without needing any runtime SPI initialization commands.

I discovered an unspoken reality in the display modding scene: while communities like [BitBuilt.net](https://bitbuilt.net/) had talented tinkerers discussing the RTD2660. When people finally got their custom panels working, they shared their findings and infos as they cant share working sources as its illegal.

## 4. Hardware Safety First: Backing Up the Factory ROM

Before touching any code, I needed a safety net.

Years ago, I had bought a cheap **CH341A USB SPI programmer** to dump and flash a laptop BIOS to unlock advanced settings. It had been sitting in a parts drawer ever since.

Took out CH341A, and dumped the full factory binary (`live_dump.bin`).

![CH341A programmer hooked to the RTD2660 SPI flash via SOIC8 test clip](assets/ch34a.jpg)

The stock flash was a Zbit **25VQ40BTJG** (4 Mbit). not knowing if the dump was proper, I went to my local hobby electronics shop and bought two **Winbond W25Q64** (64 Mbit / 8 MB) chips. desoldered the original Zbit, soldered down a fresh Winbond chip, and flashed `live_dump.bin` onto it. The board booted up perfectly. My hardware sandbox was ready.

## 5. The 8051 Abyss & The 2KB Keil Grim Reaper

The RTD2660, It’s powered by an ancient, banked **8051 microcontroller core** running at 27 MHz.

To compile **LEAKED Realtek** firmware, have to use the legacy **Keil PK51 C51 compiler**. 
- The free evaluation version of Keil has a draconian **2 KB code size limit**. The Realtek display pipeline compiles to over 80 KB across three memory banks (`BANK0`, `BANK1`, `BANK2`).
- Thankfully, [Silicon Labs offers a free full Keil PK51 license for developers](https://www.silabs.com/software-and-tools/keil-pk51), which saved me from being dead in the water.

Here was my biggest hurdle: **I had literally ZERO prior knowledge of 8051 architecture.**
- No idea how memory banking (`L51_BANK.A51`) worked.
- No idea how SFRs (Special Function Registers) were mapped.
- No idea how Realtek's indexed register access (`CScalerSetByte`, `CScalerPageSelect`) functioned.

At first, I tried asking AI models to help adapt existing open-source panel code. I copy-pasted panel structures, compiled the code, flashed it to the Winbond chip, and hit power.

**Total failure.** Completely black screen. Not even the backlight turned on.

## 6. MacGyvering Tools: ESP32 Sniffers and a Browser-Based Emulator

I opened file after file in the Realtek SDK, searching for any trace of how a SPI initialization sequence was supposed to be sent to a panel. There was nothing. All stock files assumed pure parallel video.

I needed to see what the working factory firmware was actually doing at electrical boot.

Sitting infront of me covered in dust were a few microcontrollers: an **ESP32-C3 Supermini**, an ESP32-C6, and an ESP32-S3. Ye its dusty heree.

I grabbed the **ESP32-C3 Supermini** and asked AI to compile a mmakeshift SPI logic sniffer, soldered wires to the FPC breakout board, and booted the board with the working factory firmware.

**It worked.** The ESP32 logged the raw SPI byte stream of factory firmware!

Using those SPI init CMD, I updated my custom firmware's init, flashed it, and for the first time: **the backlight lit!**

But the screen was still pitch black. Even worse, the RTD2660 chip stayed completely cold to the touch. In silicon, a cold chip means only one thing: **the 8051 CPU crashed or halted immediately after boot.**
